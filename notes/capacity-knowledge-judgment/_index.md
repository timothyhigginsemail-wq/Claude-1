# A Model of Capacity, Knowledge, and Judgment

1. **[[Intellectual ability has three distinct components: capacity, knowledge, and judgment.]]** Capacity is our biological ability to process information, recognize patterns, and reason. Knowledge is our accumulated understanding of reality. Judgment is how effectively we evaluate evidence, assess uncertainty, form conclusions, and revise beliefs.

2. **[[Capacity is biologically constrained; knowledge and judgment are developed.]]** People are born with different cognitive abilities and natural tendencies in how they think. Yet everyone begins knowing almost nothing about an extraordinarily complex world, with judgment initially driven largely by intuition. Knowledge must be acquired, and judgment must be refined through learning and experience. Neither ignorance nor limited natural ability is an intellectual failure.

3. **[[Knowledge is additive and effectively unbounded.]]** Every correct understanding adds to what we know. Some knowledge is more valuable than others, but there is no fixed ceiling on how much understanding we can accumulate. Ignorance is the baseline: knowing something earns positive credit, while not knowing it earns zero, not a deduction.

4. **[[Being wrong is a natural part of learning.]]** We necessarily rely on incomplete information and simplified models to understand the world, making incorrect beliefs unavoidable. A mistake often reflects knowledge not yet acquired rather than limited capacity or poor judgment. What matters is not simply whether a belief is correct, but how reasonably it was formed, held, and revised.

5. **[[Judgment determines how effectively we use our capacity and knowledge.]]** It involves identifying relevant evidence, reasoning logically, assigning appropriate confidence, and updating beliefs when new information emerges. High intelligence and extensive knowledge do not guarantee good judgment; both can be used to rationalize existing biases. Conversely, sound judgment requires enough knowledge to recognize what constitutes meaningful evidence.

6. **[[Poor judgment is subtractive in a way that ignorance is not.]]** Not knowing something is neutral, but unjustified certainty, flawed reasoning, and resistance to contradictory evidence warrant deductions. Someone can reach the right conclusion through poor reasoning or the wrong conclusion through sound reasoning. Judgment should therefore be evaluated by the quality of the thinking, not simply the correctness of the outcome.

7. **[[Capacity, knowledge, and judgment follow different scoring principles.]]** Capacity is constrained by biology. Knowledge is an accumulating stock of correct understanding, with no fixed scoring ceiling. Judgment is refined toward an ideal of sound reasoning and accurate calibration, where weaknesses can count against someone. The goal is neither certainty nor skepticism, but believing what the evidence supports with the confidence it warrants.
